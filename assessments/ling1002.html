Old People Don't Talk Properly Anymore

CW: mild swearing

The first ever message sent between two computers, in 1969, was lo. It was meant to be login, but the connection crashed halfway through - ironically enough, the first ever digital message was short. In 1981 the Internet Protocol was created, and thousands of new spaces formed. The speech of these early spaces, was noticeably different from Standard Written English (SWE). The main driving force was the limitations of the medium. For 'old' IRC chats:

  • - Character limits - 9 for nicknames, 512 for messages (about 100 words).
  • Text characters - only ASCII text was permitted - English letters and basic symbols.
  • Typing speed - This was the first time that text communication was truly live for the average person1.

It was important to finish your message quickly. As IRC was replaced by other methods and spaces these limitations changed, but they never truly went away - you can imagine how these limitations led to shortening (especially with mobile phone keyboards that really don't like punctuation). People also began to realise that many of those little conventions we have in standard writing - capitals, full stops, apostrophes, and so on - aren't actually that necessary in these short messages. They're great for readability in long texts, but in messages like these: 

[00:58:16] <Ronaldo9> xploits , just how lucky are u ?
[00:58:42] <Xsploit> im 25 and i live with my mum
[00:58:46] <Xsploit> does that answer your question?

They wouldn't actually contribute very much. Now that people weren't using these features conventionally, we could assign new meanings: 

(18:50:14) <Mihawk> HAHA YES
(18:50:21) <Mihawk> My girlfriends turtle finally died

Capitals are shouting and a punctuation sets a tone - Ok is neutral but Ok. is a conversation-ender2. Note that ? and ! are still useful, and we've kept those.

Digital Natives

Around this time, we see the first of the 'digital natives' - the people who grew up with technology.3 This group grew up with access to text messaging with fast smartphone keyboards. Unlike those who came before, digital communication wasn't just a poor substitute, but a true medium for conversation that would develop in its own direction. Many of this generation learned to type as they learned to speak, and text all the time. Give this a few decades to develop and we see some real deviation. This language might seem strange, but it is real and meaningful5 . It's the natural conclusion of the development we saw earlier - the language has 'simplified' (we use smaller words), and especially younger users drop punctuation and capitals - This isn't laziness4 and it can actually lead to better literacy in children and young adults, and many seem to prefer using and reading it.Many of the younger generation even express frustration with the 'limits' of SWE compared to this variant especially with emotive conversations (anecdotally, though the pattern exists). That's to say nothing of the new 'Algospeak' - talking in code, intentional misspelling, satire and innuendo to dodge censorship on corporate social media or by Governments.

This is Real Language 

Nah ts pmo sm rn nglicl u pmo n...

The beginning of a very emotional message I was shown by a friend - It's a tad ridiculous5, but I can't imagine how SWE would express this level of directionless frustration in a 'proper' way

It's a different system, but it's still governed by rules and conventions the same way that SWE is - to elevate one form over another, or to call one form 'lazy' or 'incorrect' seems ridiculous. Just like how “ng tspmo rn” (On god, this shit piss(es) me off right now) carries quite a different meaning in an academic paper, “That's ok.” in a text message doesn't mean quite what it does somewhere else - each has its place, and using one in another's territory isn't proper in either case. Language is always changing and evolving, and lamenting this change only means that you'll be left behind. It seems that the only way forward is to learn to understand this language, and its place in our world. In doing so, we might be able to appreciate its unique abilities and its drawbacks, and the beautiful new forms of communication it can facilitate.
